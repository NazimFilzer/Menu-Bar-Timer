# Eye-break recommendations and evidence

Research checked: 19 October 2026. Scope: AAO/AOA patient guidance and original studies directly assessing the 20-20-20 rule; not a systematic review or individualized medical advice. This is a research note, not an implementation decision.

## Practical recommendation

**During sustained screen/near work, every 20 minutes, look away at an object at least 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for at least 20 seconds.** This matches the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO), which explicitly uses “at least” for distance and duration, and the American Optometric Association (AOA), which recommends a 20-second distant-viewing break every 20 minutes. The interval refers to screen viewing, not a 20-minute rest period. [1][2]

| Parameter | Guidance-supported value | Evidence qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interval | Every 20 minutes of screen viewing | A practical professional recommendation, not an experimentally established optimum. [1–4] |
| Break duration | At least 20 seconds | AAO wording; original trials have not consistently demonstrated that 20 seconds relieves symptoms. [1][3][4] |
| Viewing distance | At least 20 feet; approximately 6 metres | AAO wording. This is the distant target, **not** monitor distance. Neither intervention abstract establishes that exactly 20 feet is a necessary or optimal threshold. [1][3][4] |
| Longer rest | AOA also suggests resting eyes for 15 minutes after two hours of continuous computer use | Separate advice alongside the short breaks; not evidence that 20-second breaks replace longer rests. The two direct intervention studies below do not establish this longer-break dose. [2–4] |

The AAO’s separate computer-use article also recommends occasionally looking up and away, frequent blinking, glare/brightness adjustments and positioning the monitor around arm’s length away. AOA similarly treats rest breaks as one part of ergonomics and appropriate vision correction. Looking at a nearby phone is not the distant-viewing action specified by the rule. [1][2][5]

## What original studies found

### Talens-Estarelles et al.: real-world reminders, symptom improvement

*The effects of breaks on digital eye strain, dry eye and binocular vision: Testing the 20-20-20 rule* appeared online in August 2022 and in *Contact Lens and Anterior Eye* 46(2), 101744 (2023). [3]

- **Design:** 29 symptomatic computer users installed laptop software using a webcam to assess breaks, gaze and blinking, with personalized 20-20-20 reminders. Measurements were taken before and after two weeks of reminders and one week after stopping. This is a small before/after intervention; the abstract describes no parallel randomized control group. [3]
- **Results:** Digital eye-strain and dry-eye symptom scores decreased during use (reported p ≤ 0.045), but the improvement was not maintained one week after discontinuation. No ocular-surface or tear-film parameter changed significantly (p ≥ 0.089). Binocular measures generally did not change, except accommodative facility (p = 0.010). [3]
- **Limits:** Short follow-up and a small symptomatic sample limit generalization. Computer-work duration and break behavior also changed, so the result does not isolate the effect of exactly 20 minutes, 20 seconds or 20 feet. Subjective improvement is not evidence of repairing dry-eye signs or preventing permanent eye damage. The abstract provides significance tests but not enough effect-size information to quantify a clinically meaningful benefit here. [3]

### Johnson and Rosenfield: short laboratory tasks, no detected benefit

*20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?* appeared online in December 2022 and in *Optometry and Vision Science* 100(1), 52–56 (2023). [4]

- **Design:** 30 young participants completed a demanding 40-minute tablet reading task on four separate occasions. Twenty-second breaks were allowed every 5, 10, 20 or 40 minutes (the 40-minute condition meant no break during the task). Symptoms, reading speed and accuracy were assessed. [4]
- **Results:** Symptoms increased after all four trials (p < 0.001). Scheduled breaks had no significant effect on symptoms (p = 0.70), reading speed (p = 0.93) or accuracy (p = 0.55). The authors did not support 20-second scheduled breaks as a therapeutic intervention under these conditions. [4]
- **Limits:** A short laboratory task in young people does not settle effectiveness over a full working day, with longer breaks, or in other clinical populations. Failure to detect benefit is not proof that all eye breaks are ineffective. The abstract does not provide enough detail to verify actual distant-target placement or independently test distance as a variable. [4]

### Datta et al.: observational context, not an efficacy trial

A survey of 432 participants, published in *Indian Journal of Ophthalmology* 71(5), 2071–2075 (2023), found that 34% practiced the rule regularly or occasionally. Overall symptom scores did not differ significantly between practicing and nonpracticing groups (p = 0.53); those with burning or headache were more likely to practice it. Self-selection, self-reported practice, no clinical measurements and uncertain adherence mean this survey cannot show that the rule causes improvement or harm. [6]

## Strength of evidence and appropriate wording

**Professional guidance is clear; direct efficacy evidence is limited and mixed.** One small real-world reminder study found short-term symptom improvement, while one small repeated-task laboratory study did not. They differ in participants, setting and exposure, so neither simply cancels the other. This is a qualitative appraisal, not a formal GRADE rating. [1–4]

These sources support offering a simple distant-viewing reminder, but do **not** establish an optimal interval/duration/distance, a guaranteed benefit, a treatment for every cause of eye strain, or prevention of myopia, retinal damage or permanent vision loss. The directly assessed outcomes were predominantly symptoms and short-term ocular measures, not long-term disease prevention. [3][4]

Suggested product language, if a feature is later considered: **“Look away: focus on something at least 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. Eye-care organizations recommend regular distance breaks; research on the exact 20-20-20 schedule is limited.”** This is an editorial suggestion based on the guidance and evidence above, not a tested intervention. [1–4]

Persistent symptoms warrant professional assessment rather than simply increasing reminders. AAO advises consulting an ophthalmologist if strain persists after workspace changes, and its computer-use guidance flags persistent redness, blur, watering, light sensitivity or pain for assessment. [1][5]
